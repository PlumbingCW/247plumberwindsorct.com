Building Water Leak Detection in Windsor, CT

A hidden leak in a commercial or multi-unit building can run for weeks before anyone notices. Here is how managers and owners find it.

Call (860) 397-8103

By the Windsor 24/7 Plumbing team · Published November 12, 2026 · Updated November 12, 2026

Building leaks do not announce themselves. A slow leak behind a wall or under a slab can run for weeks. The first signs are often indirect: a water bill that climbs, a meter that moves when the building is empty or a stain that appears on the ceiling below. For managers of offices, retail spaces and multi-unit residential buildings, finding the leak early limits damage and cost.

Warning signs at the building level

What managers should watch

  • ✓
    A rising water bill Compare consumption against past months and occupancy.
  • ✓
    A moving meter after hours Read the meter with the building empty and all equipment off.
  • ✓
    Stains, bubbling paint or warped flooring Water is moving behind finishes.
  • ✓
    Musty smell or visible mold Moisture has been present for a while.
  • ✓
    Low pressure on upper floors A leak or a failing valve in a riser.

A step-by-step approach

Start with the meter. Shut off all water use that you can, wait, and read it again. If it moves, there is a leak somewhere between the meter and the fixtures. Then isolate zones with shutoff valves. Close one zone at a time and watch the meter to see which zone is losing water.

Once you have a zone, a plumber narrows it down with listening equipment, thermal imaging and pressure testing. The aim is to mark the spot so that an opening is small and precise.

Common leak sources in commercial and multi-unit buildings

Failed supply lines at fixtures, leaking risers in shared walls, heating and cooling condensate lines, water heaters, mop sinks and failed valves are frequent culprits. Drain leaks are also common, particularly stacks in older buildings. Radiant and hydronic heating loops can also leak under slabs or in walls.

Slab leaks need acoustic and thermal testing, and some leaks are in drain pipes, where a camera shows the problem. Our commercial plumbing page covers what we handle for businesses.

Buildings and water in Windsor

Windsor's Day Hill Road corridor is a major corporate and industrial office area, and Windsor is an MDC member town. A large building draws a lot of water, which makes a meter-based leak check practical. Ask the MDC how to read your meter, and check whether your property has a backflow preventer and when it was last tested.

The town ordinance restricts new structures in 100-year floodplains, and the town's hazard documents list flood-prone areas including River Street. For buildings near brooks or the Farmington River, a wet basement or lower level may be groundwater instead of a pipe leak. Leak detection separates the two.

What to document

Write down meter readings and times, photograph stains, note which units or rooms are affected and keep any failed parts. Tell your insurer about suspected leaks early, since policies often treat sudden and long-term leaks differently. Share your notes with the plumber. They speed up the search.

If tenants are involved, communicate clearly about shutoffs and access. A leak that goes unreported by tenants for weeks costs everyone.

Plan ahead

A building with a good shutoff map, labeled valves and a yearly walk-through finds leaks sooner. Annual inspections of water heaters, mechanical rooms and fixtures catch problems while they are small.

A monthly leak check for building managers

Read the meter on the same day each month and log the number with the date. Walk the mechanical rooms, restrooms and janitor closets and look for drips, stains and corrosion. Listen at water heaters and under sinks. Run a flashlight along ceilings below restrooms and kitchens.

Test shutoff valves once a year, so you know which ones still close. Label them. When a leak happens, a clear shutoff map is worth a lot. Keep a short list of the contractors you call, and tell tenants what to report and to whom.

Working with tenants and staff

Tell tenants and staff what a leak looks like and who to call. Give them a short list of signs, such as stains, drips, odors and unusual sounds, and ask them to report promptly. Thank people who report early. A culture that reports small problems prevents large ones.

For multi-unit buildings, include access instructions in your lease or house rules so plumbers can reach shutoffs and wall openings quickly. A leak that needs a key nobody can find becomes a bigger leak.
