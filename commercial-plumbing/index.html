Emergencies that stop a business

The common emergencies are sewer backups, burst supply lines, failed water heaters, overflowing grease interceptors and flooded floors. If sewage is backing up, stop using the restrooms and drains and keep customers out of the affected area. If a supply line has burst, close the main shutoff or the zone valve.

Contact your landlord or property manager if you lease. Responsibility for plumbing differs from lease to lease, and we can document what we find for them.

Grease traps and interceptors

Kitchens produce grease that cools and solidifies in drain lines. A grease trap or interceptor captures it before it reaches the sewer. When the trap is full, grease passes through and clogs the line or causes odors. Regular cleaning keeps the trap working, and your local sewer authority may have requirements about frequency and records.

We clean traps on a schedule that fits your volume, and we jet the downstream lines when buildup requires it. See hydro jetting for how we clear heavy grease.

Backflow prevention and testing

A backflow preventer keeps contaminated water from flowing back into the public supply. Many commercial buildings need one on fire lines, irrigation and process equipment, and most require periodic testing by a certified tester. Rules vary by utility and building type, so ask your water provider what applies to your property.

Windsor's water provider is the MDC. If you are not sure whether your building has a backflow device or when it was last tested, call us and we will help you find out.

Storm drains and floor drains

Parking lots and loading docks drain through catch basins that collect leaves, silt and trash. When they clog, water ponds and can enter the building. We clean catch basins and trench drains, and we jet the lines. Floor drains inside warehouses and kitchens need regular attention too.

Windsor's Day Hill Road corridor is a corporate and industrial office area. If you manage a property there, a seasonal drain check before fall rains is easy to schedule.

Planned work and service plans

We handle remodels, fixture changes and water heater replacement in commercial buildings. See plumbing installation and water heater replacement. For ongoing needs, ask about scheduled inspections and cleaning, so problems get caught during business hours instead of at night.

Keeping records and avoiding repeat calls

A business that logs its plumbing events sees patterns. Note the date, location and cause of each backup or leak, and who handled it. After two or three entries, a line that keeps clogging stands out, and the conversation shifts from emergency cleaning to a lasting fix.

Ask for a camera record of lateral and floor drain lines in older buildings, and a map of shutoff valves for each floor or suite. Share both with your staff or property manager, so the person on duty at night knows what to close.

The standard commercial work follows

Commercial plumbing carries added requirements for backflow, fixture counts and access. Connecticut's State Building Code uses the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as its plumbing standard. The 2022 state code adopts the 2021 IPC, and the state is working on a newer edition. Ask the Windsor building department which edition and permits apply to your building. Source: Connecticut Office of the State Building Inspector.

What to do while you wait for a plumber

Close the nearest shutoff, and the building main if water is spreading. Move people away from wet floors and anything electrical. Do not use a toilet that is backing up, and do not mix cleaning chemicals to clear a drain. Note the time the problem began and which fixtures are affected.

If sewage is present, keep staff and customers out of the area. If you smell gas, leave and call 911. Photograph the damage for your insurer, and keep your tenant or landlord informed. When to call 911 instead of a plumber: gas smell, a person in danger or water touching live electrical equipment.
